## AC1.1 — Assess the relationship between an organisation's strategic objectives and operational planning
*Currently ~560 words — slightly over budget, trim at final pass*

### The gap between knowing and doing

Before this programme, work on inclusion focused on frameworks and pedagogy rather than on the practicalities of making learning materials accessible. The analogy I use is a kitchen: we all know how to clean one and we all understand the benefits of a clean kitchen, but we don't enjoy doing it, so we stop, and eventually the kitchen is filthy. Accessibility is much the same. We know that headings should be structured, that images need alt text, that digital resources should be readable by a screen reader. We know about Immersive Reader. But we do not consistently do what is necessary to enable learners who need assistive technology to actually use it.

A typical example is that staff tell me they know about Immersive Reader. When I ask whether their students know about it, since they are the ones who need it, the answer is usually no. When I follow up by asking whether their resources are built so that students can use assistive technology effectively, the answer is usually "how do I do that?"

The college has done substantial work on the SEND Framework and Quality First Teaching. What has been missing is attention to the detail and the foundations that allow those things to work. This is what the Jisc Discovery question set in December, and the Digital Lead audits in July and August, have made visible.

### Translating the objective into deliverables

Making TLA inclusive for all has been translated into a set of specific, deliverable requirements. Staff produce learning materials that are accessible by design. Digital learning environments are easy to navigate and reduce the number of clicks needed to reach learning material. Learning content and teaching moments are captured digitally to support learner independence, which builds on accessible design rather than replacing it. Alongside this sits how materials are shared with learners, and how safe and communicative learning environments are maintained through Microsoft Teams.

I have also developed resources for both learners and staff on the assistive technology already available on their own devices, whether phones, tablets, laptops or computers: Read&Write, dictation in settings, Immersive Reader, and closed captions on video. Both groups are necessary. Accessible materials have limited value if learners do not know the tools exist, and knowing the tools has limited value if the materials will not work with them. Providing for both means staff can pass the information on confidently, and learners who need assistive technology can use it against materials designed to support it.

### The feedback loop

The findings feed back into the strategic picture through three levels of reporting. I report to my line manager, the Assistant Principal for Quality, on the detail: the work undertaken, what has been identified, the action plans proposed, the impact, and specifically who I have worked with. I report to the Vice Principal for Quality on impact at a collegiate level: the scale of the problem, RAG ratings, how many areas have completed the audit, which areas need most support, what is planned for them, and what impact previous work has had.

I also have upcoming meetings with the Deputy Principals responsible for learner experience and learner outcomes. These discussions extend beyond improving the accessibility of learning materials. They address the need for non-teaching roles, including the executive team and Professional Learner Services, to receive training on accessibility and inclusion, so that the foundations for the SEND Framework are in place across every part of the organisation rather than in teaching alone.

This illustrates that the relationship between strategic objectives and operational planning runs in both directions. The objective set the direction, but the operational data has changed the understanding of what achieving it requires.

## AC1.2 — Evaluate the use of approaches to operational planning
*~450 words — on budget*

I deliberately designed this programme around Deming's Plan–Do–Check–Act cycle. Its strength is that it supports iterative development, which suits how I work: I develop plans, strategies, ideas and resources iteratively, based on reflection, feedback and evidence of impact. It is slower than a linear approach, but measuring against a baseline set of data gives clarity about where impact has been achieved, and identifies where action will have the greatest effect.

I have worked with Gantt charts before, and I still plan on the principle of building phases towards a target. This helps me stay on top of progress and deadlines, and allows measurement at the right milestone points. Its limitation is that it assumes a degree of control I do not have. Teaching timetables, the availability of other departments, and factors outside my remit all affect the sequence.

The programme also relies on resource and capacity planning. I cannot do this work alone across 600 staff, so building a network of 36 volunteer Digital Leads extends reach across a far wider scope of the college. The limitation is obvious: they are volunteers with existing teaching commitments, and engagement will vary. Even if only half work enthusiastically where I need them to, this significantly reduces my workload and helps the college move more quickly towards the foundations needed to support the SEND Framework and the inclusive practice identified in the Ofsted toolkit. Put simply, even one volunteer working well extends my reach and accelerates impact.

Of the three, capacity planning is the approach that carries the programme. I recognised early that it would be impossible to understand the position of the college on accessibility and inclusion without first building a network of volunteers who could help identify what the landscape actually looked like, what the specific issues were, and what an action plan for their area and staff should contain. It is the capacity planning that makes Deming's PDCA cycle possible at all; without the network there is no way to gather the data that the "check" stage depends on.

This has also proved differentiated in practice. Some Digital Leads receive an action plan reflecting their own audit findings, with guidance on improvements, and are content to run with it themselves. Others need me to talk through the process, the patterns in their data and the actions arising before they implement. Others need me to be directly involved in training and development with their staff. Capacity planning has therefore allowed me to build a picture across the college far more rapidly than I could alone, while also identifying where Digital Leads can take ownership in their own areas and grow into leadership roles.
