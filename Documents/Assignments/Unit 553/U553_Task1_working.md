## AC1.1 — Assess the relationship between an organisation's strategic objectives and operational planning
*Currently ~560 words — slightly over budget, trim at final pass*

### The gap between knowing and doing

Before this programme, work on inclusion focused on frameworks and pedagogy rather than on the practicalities of making learning materials accessible. The analogy I use is a kitchen: we all know how to clean one and we all understand the benefits of a clean kitchen, but we don't enjoy doing it, so we stop, and eventually the kitchen is filthy. Accessibility is much the same. We know that headings should be structured, that images need alt text, that digital resources should be readable by a screen reader. We know about Immersive Reader. But we do not consistently do what is necessary to enable learners who need assistive technology to actually use it.

A typical example is that staff tell me they know about Immersive Reader. When I ask whether their students know about it, since they are the ones who need it, the answer is usually no. When I follow up by asking whether their resources are built so that students can use assistive technology effectively, the answer is usually "how do I do that?"

The college has done substantial work on the SEND Framework and Quality First Teaching. What has been missing is attention to the detail and the foundations that allow those things to work. This is what the Jisc Discovery question set in December, and the Digital Lead audits in July and August, have made visible.

### Translating the objective into deliverables

Making TLA inclusive for all has been translated into a set of specific, deliverable requirements. Staff produce learning materials that are accessible by design. Digital learning environments are easy to navigate and reduce the number of clicks needed to reach learning material. Learning content and teaching moments are captured digitally to support learner independence, which builds on accessible design rather than replacing it. Alongside this sits how materials are shared with learners, and how safe and communicative learning environments are maintained through Microsoft Teams.

I have also developed resources for both learners and staff on the assistive technology already available on their own devices, whether phones, tablets, laptops or computers: Read&Write, dictation in settings, Immersive Reader, and closed captions on video. Both groups are necessary. Accessible materials have limited value if learners do not know the tools exist, and knowing the tools has limited value if the materials will not work with them. Providing for both means staff can pass the information on confidently, and learners who need assistive technology can use it against materials designed to support it.

### The feedback loop

The findings feed back into the strategic picture through three levels of reporting. I report to my line manager, the Assistant Principal for Quality, on the detail: the work undertaken, what has been identified, the action plans proposed, the impact, and specifically who I have worked with. I report to the Vice Principal for Quality on impact at a collegiate level: the scale of the problem, RAG ratings, how many areas have completed the audit, which areas need most support, what is planned for them, and what impact previous work has had.

I also have upcoming meetings with the Deputy Principals responsible for learner experience and learner outcomes. These discussions extend beyond improving the accessibility of learning materials. They address the need for non-teaching roles, including the executive team and Professional Learner Services, to receive training on accessibility and inclusion, so that the foundations for the SEND Framework are in place across every part of the organisation rather than in teaching alone.

This illustrates that the relationship between strategic objectives and operational planning runs in both directions. The objective set the direction, but the operational data has changed the understanding of what achieving it requires.

## AC1.2 — Evaluate the use of approaches to operational planning
*~450 words — on budget*

I deliberately designed this programme around Deming's Plan–Do–Check–Act cycle. Its strength is that it supports iterative development, which suits how I work: I develop plans, strategies, ideas and resources iteratively, based on reflection, feedback and evidence of impact. It is slower than a linear approach, but measuring against a baseline set of data gives clarity about where impact has been achieved, and identifies where action will have the greatest effect.

I have worked with Gantt charts before, and I still plan on the principle of building phases towards a target. This helps me stay on top of progress and deadlines, and allows measurement at the right milestone points. Its limitation is that it assumes a degree of control I do not have. Teaching timetables, the availability of other departments, and factors outside my remit all affect the sequence.

The programme also relies on resource and capacity planning. I cannot do this work alone across 600 staff, so building a network of 36 volunteer Digital Leads extends reach across a far wider scope of the college. The limitation is obvious: they are volunteers with existing teaching commitments, and engagement will vary. Even if only half work enthusiastically where I need them to, this significantly reduces my workload and helps the college move more quickly towards the foundations needed to support the SEND Framework and the inclusive practice identified in the Ofsted toolkit. Put simply, even one volunteer working well extends my reach and accelerates impact.

Of the three, capacity planning is the approach that carries the programme. I recognised early that it would be impossible to understand the position of the college on accessibility and inclusion without first building a network of volunteers who could help identify what the landscape actually looked like, what the specific issues were, and what an action plan for their area and staff should contain. It is the capacity planning that makes Deming's PDCA cycle possible at all; without the network there is no way to gather the data that the "check" stage depends on.

This has also proved differentiated in practice. Some Digital Leads receive an action plan reflecting their own audit findings, with guidance on improvements, and are content to run with it themselves. Others need me to talk through the process, the patterns in their data and the actions arising before they implement. Others need me to be directly involved in training and development with their staff. Capacity planning has therefore allowed me to build a picture across the college far more rapidly than I could alone, while also identifying where Digital Leads can take ownership in their own areas and grow into leadership roles.

## AC2.1 — Assess methods of managing and leading the delivery of an operational plan
*~500 words — on budget*

To manage and lead delivery of the plan I have used a differentiated model, with the Digital Leads at the centre of driving the programme. It follows Hersey and Blanchard's situational leadership model, matching my approach to each volunteer's readiness and capability in the role: directing someone new to the role, coaching them as they develop, supporting them when they are capable but hesitant, and delegating when they are both able and willing. Hersey, P. and Blanchard, K.H. (1993) Management of Organizational Behavior: Utilizing Human Resources, Prentice Hall, Englewood Cliffs NJ. Its limitations are that it assumes I can recognise readiness accurately, and that it is demanding on my time, since I am running several styles at once.

The main problem I encounter is that I am not a line manager within this programme, so there is less accountability. Where this matters I lean on the leadership above me: my own line manager, who leads on quality improvement, and the Vice Principal for Quality. With their support, if an action plan is not followed through, or is not being implemented by a team, there is a route through Heads of Area, who do hold line management responsibility for the Digital Leads, to ensure the actions are carried out.

Visibility is deliberately built into the process for this reason. I report Health Check completion rates and RAG ratings by area to the Vice Principal for Quality. I am uncomfortable with the idea of reporting on colleagues, but patterns become obvious when work has been asked for and not done, and making that visible at a level above the Head of Area means accountability does not have to rest on me personally. Those with the authority to act can then direct what I do next, allowing me to carry their name in my communications, or engage someone else with the appropriate authority to act on my behalf.

My preferred approach reflects the leadership style identified through Insights Discovery. I work collaboratively to find where barriers exist and support people in removing them, rather than leading with direct authority. In previous roles I have found this generates better buy-in and more sustained impact, and that building relationships this way allows colleagues and me to work more innovatively.

Contact varies. Some Digital Leads I meet more regularly than others, but I communicate with all of them at least weekly or fortnightly to understand where they are, what issues or barriers have arisen, and what their reality looks like. This means that if a task has not been completed, I already understand the context. A department may be carrying staff absence, with everyone covering additional workload, so lower-priority tasks get pushed back. Understanding the day-to-day context of Digital Leads in their departments helps me judge what is realistic rather than simply what was expected.

My intention is to report back to Digital Leads directly, so that they can report to their own Heads of Area. This raises their profile and supports their development into leadership pathways, while allowing a differentiated approach: a lighter touch with those who are more experienced, and a stronger coaching role with those who are less so.

## AC2.2 — Review techniques for problem solving and decision making
*~560 words — trim at final pass if needed*

**Root cause analysis.** Throughout the planning and discovery phase I identified that accessibility was a problem, but I made assumptions about the root cause. I assumed staff knew the basics and simply were not doing them, and that this was down to a lack of time or slipping into bad habits. What the audit revealed is different. Staff regularly told me they understood accessibility and did not need training, but further enquiry showed they were not meeting basic standards. We know the language of accessibility and inclusion, but not the functions that make something accessible, or how to share resources so that learners can use assistive technology against them. We know Immersive Reader exists and we promote it, but the next step is missed: how the learner reaches the material, and whether the resource has been built so that it works well when assistive technology is used. My conclusion is that we have oversimplified accessible and inclusive practice, and are now in danger of overcomplicating something fundamental. Immersive Reader is covered in induction in September and never revisited. This matters more now because increasing numbers of learners arrive from secondary school with poor digital literacy, many having stopped using computers after Year 9, so a single introduction is not enough.

**Data-driven decision making.** The Digital Health Check audit exists precisely so that decisions are not made on best guesses. The data tells me what the issue is, where it is, what action is needed and how urgently. In some departments I have offered whole-team training; in others I have suggested the Digital Lead puts forward a named member of staff for half an hour of one-to-one coaching on designing accessible materials.

**Prioritisation and triage.** I built a system to RAG rate areas so I can prioritise where the greatest impact is available. I am currently focusing on two. English, because their learners are largely retaking GCSEs having not achieved a grade 4 at secondary school, and are therefore learners who may already have faced barriers. Where we provide digitally inaccessible material, or material that induces cognitive overload, we add another barrier, and the likely response is to disengage. Redesigning that material makes curiosity and confidence more likely. The second is SEND, whose expert knowledge of supporting learners with additional needs is exceptional, but whose own materials and communications are often inaccessible. Given their reach across the college and the organisational objective that the SEND Framework permeates every part of it, that work would be foundational.

**Consultative decision making.** I have worked with Jisc and the community I belong to within it to refine my approach, particularly learning from others attempting to drive organisational change without authority or accountability metrics: how to nudge people towards chosen behaviours rather than instruct them. This approach is consistent with Thaler and Sunstein's concept of choice architecture, where behaviour is influenced by how options are presented rather than by mandate. Thaler, R.H. and Sunstein, C.R. (2008) Nudge: Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth and Happiness, Yale University Press, New Haven. Alongside SEND consultants and my own research, this informs the decisions I take.

**Iterative review.** The PDCA cycle runs throughout, keeping the programme reflective and checking that what we are doing remains fit for purpose rather than continuing down a road because it looked right at the outset. This matters because external factors — Ofsted inspection, departmental restructuring — sit outside my control and carry higher priority than this programme.

## AC2.3 — Assess the ethical requirements that influence an operational plan
*~600 words — trim at final pass if needed*

**Equality of access.** The most significant ethical requirement is equality of access. Framed as making learning materials accessible by design, it can sound like a nice-to-have. Framed the other way round — understanding why those learners need assistive technology in the first place — the question becomes why we are not treating it as an absolute priority that everything we produce is built with them in mind. We are currently failing our most vulnerable learners, whether through time pressure, poor habits, or lack of knowledge. For me this is foundational: everything else sits above it, and until it is right, the work above it is compromised. That is not the current reality, but it is the central ethical issue in this plan.

There is also a legal dimension. The Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications) (No. 2) Accessibility Regulations 2018 apply to further education institutions, which are considered in scope through their dependence on public funding. Significantly, the regulations extend beyond public-facing websites to intranets, extranets, virtual learning environments and the digital content held on them, including documents, presentations and eLearning resources. The standard required is WCAG 2.1 AA. Content published before 23 September 2019 is exempt until it undergoes substantial revision, which means that material staff create or update now falls within scope. These regulations sit alongside the Equality Act 2010 duty to make anticipatory reasonable adjustments. Jisc (no date) Accessibility regulations – what you need to know. Available at: https://www.jisc.ac.uk/guides/accessibility-regulations-what-you-need-to-know (Accessed: 30 August 2026). I have had recent discussions with leaders who were not aware of these requirements, which is itself an ethical issue: we cannot expect staff to meet standards that the organisation has not clearly communicated.

**Reporting on colleagues.** I find it difficult to report on colleagues without first having done everything I can to support them. This is one reason I keep communication lines open and offer help consistently and informally. It is also practical. When I hand a visible report to the Vice Principal and am asked why a department has completed none of the required actions, I need to be able to answer that question. So the obligation runs two ways: supporting colleagues to meet the requirements so that their departments and learners benefit and the organisational objective is advanced, and ensuring I cannot fairly be accused of failing to support colleagues over whom I have no management responsibility.

**The volunteer relationship.** The Digital Lead role is voluntary, but it was put to Heads of Area that someone in their team would need to take it on, and that if nobody did, it would fall to them. It is unpaid, which creates an obligation to invest in those people in other ways. There are opportunities to attend educational conferences on digital learning in London and Birmingham, and ten members of staff will be offered coaching qualifications. I am also developing recognition: branded items such as hoodies or lanyard badges, a logo for email signatures, presence in the monthly staff newsletter, and a space on our internal SharePoint identifying the Digital Leads and showcasing their work.

Alongside this, I want them to understand the significance of what they are doing. The way I explained it to one group drew on my previous work as a sound engineer: if I did not do my job with integrity and care, the artists on stage would not sound good. Nobody in the audience recognises the sound engineer's work, but it is the sound engineer who makes everything else work.

**Honesty in the baseline.** I made clear in the baseline audit that we wanted to see the true state of things, including everything that was uncomfortable. An audit designed to produce reassuring results would be worthless, and would remove any ability to demonstrate improvement later. Being honest about the starting position is what allows even small improvements to be visible and meaningful.
