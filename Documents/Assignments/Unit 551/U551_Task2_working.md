## Introduction

**Drafted — 265 words of prose (budget 250).**

The Ofsted inspection toolkit is the external driver for this project. Digital skills appear repeatedly within it, and always alongside English and mathematics, including within the graded Achievement descriptors, where inspectors evaluate leaders' actions to secure learners' development of those skills (Ofsted (2025) *Further education and skills inspection toolkit*, updated 9 September 2025, Ofsted, Manchester, p. 41). Weston College currently holds no baseline of digital skills for either staff or learners. That absence is the problem this project addresses.

The learner position is widely misread. We assume arriving learners are digital natives. They are not. Their competence comes from consumer mobile applications rather than from being taught to communicate, organise material or collaborate in digital spaces. Elements appear in some curriculum areas, but a learner entering the first year of further education has most likely come through a system that stopped teaching this at the end of Year 9. The staff position mirrors it. If staff do not hold the skills, the gap simply relocates, because the college is now responsible for developing both general and industry-specific digital skills so learners can progress to employment or further study.

The immediate trigger was structural. Following a restructure of the digital learning team, the college lost its Digital Education Manager, and with that post it lost the person who translated the Ofsted toolkit into college practice and who held the relationships with Jisc, Century and other external partners. No one remained with responsibility for understanding the digital skills position of staff or learners. Across 600+ staff, 35 curriculum areas and nine campuses, that gap needed closing quickly.

## Project Objectives (AC3.1)

**Drafted — 103 words of prose plus table (budget 180). Table excluded from count.**

The project has four objectives. Where dates and volume targets were previously set, several have been suspended by the pause; the targets below apply from the point the Jisc Discovery tool is released, anticipated within the coming months. Progression is not measured by diagnostic alone: staff development is also tracked through learning walks, student voice, staff surveys and digital health checks, while the RAG rating of learner progress is owned by the Personal Development team.

| Objective | Measure | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Baseline staff digital capability across curriculum areas | Completion of the Jisc Discovery tool, with staff retaining their own data and sharing only their development priorities | Approximately two thirds of staff completing within the first month of release; reassessment annually |
| 2. Baseline learner digital skills on entry | Century diagnostic completion and subsequent progression data | Over 80% of 16–19 study programme learners baselined |
| 3. Produce the Digital Skills Framework, mapping industry-aligned digital skills to each curriculum area | Framework published and adopted, with baselining and monitoring methods defined per area | All 35 curriculum areas mapped |
| 4. Establish progression tracking so learner development is evidenced over time | Progression reported by Heads of Area through the existing Quality Handbook assurance process | Progression against each area's own intended methods reported by year end |

Objectives 1 and 2 are constrained by data protection law and by the ethical position set out above: the diagnostic exists to direct development, not to assess individuals.

## Methodology / Approach (AC3.3)

**Drafted — 314 words (budget 330).**

The project is run through an adaptive, phased methodology grounded in the continuous Plan–Do–Check–Act cycle (Deming, W.E. (1986) *Out of the Crisis*, MIT Press, Cambridge MA). Rather than a rigid college-wide rollout, the tiered delivery model allows interventions to be tested, data gathered, and the Framework refined iteratively before scaling.

The project generates a deliberate mixture of quantitative and qualitative data. Quantitatively, it draws on individual staff outputs from the Jisc Discovery tool, learner baseline diagnostics from the Century platform, and departmental health check scores mapped into the area RAG ratings. Qualitatively, learning walks, coaching conversations and student voice feedback supply the context the numbers cannot. Neither stream is sufficient alone: a score identifies where to look, a conversation explains what is actually happening there.

Because auditing digital capability triggers staff anxiety, storage and access are governed by a strict staff-hold-their-own-data model. This is a methodological decision taken to protect psychological safety, not merely a compliance measure. Individual staff retain complete ownership of their Jisc Discovery profiles. Management and project leads see only anonymised, aggregated data at departmental level, never individual performance metrics. The only individual-level information shared with me is the direction a member of staff wishes to develop in and the priorities they have set for themselves. Learner diagnostic data held on Century is managed under role-based access controls, so that only the educators directly responsible for those learners can view it, maintaining UK GDPR compliance.

Raw data from these streams is collated using securely hosted internal tools, principally dedicated SharePoint pages, and analysed to build highly specific profiles for individual academic and support departments. Data is not collected for reporting alone; it drives what happens next. Analysing area profiles translates raw data into targeted action, deploying coaching resource precisely where the operational gaps sit, rather than relying on generic blanket training that never addresses a specific need.
